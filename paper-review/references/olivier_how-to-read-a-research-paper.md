If research papers feel dense or slow-going, you're not alone. Reading a paper in three passes makes it easier to digest. With each read, you go a little deeper than the last, so the big picture comes first, then the details. You can stop after any pass once you have what you need. As you read, keep one question in mind:

> What would need to be true for me to believe this conclusion?

The three-pass approach comes from computer scientist Srinivasan Keshav, whose short paper "How to read a paper" (2007) has been adapted by researchers in many fields.

Tools like Consensus can help you find the best papers for your question. This guide covers what to do once you've found them.

## 01. The anatomy of a paper

Most scientific papers follow a similar structure. Each section answers a question, and knowing that question tells you what to look for.

| Section      | The question it answers                     | Look for                                                                     |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Title        | What is this about?                         | The topic, setting and type of question                                      |
| Abstract     | What is the whole paper, in brief?          | Problem, hypothesis, method, main findings                                   |
| Introduction | Why did they do this?                       | The gap in knowledge the paper fills                                         |
| Methods      | How did they do it?                         | Who or what was studied, how data were collected, choices that shape results |
| Results      | What did they find?                         | Sample size, key numbers, tables and figures                                 |
| Discussion   | What does it mean, and what are the limits? | Interpretation, comparison to past work, stated limitations                  |
| Conclusion   | What should I take away?                    | The practical bottom line                                                    |
| References   | What is this built on?                      | Key earlier studies worth reading next                                       |

Also glance at the authors, their affiliations, the funding source and any conflict of interest statement. Remember that the abstract is a summary written by the authors, not the evidence itself. It highlights what they want you to notice and leaves out most of the caveats.

## 02. How paper types differ

The three passes work for any paper, but what to check first depends on the type.

| Paper type                        | How it differs                                                      | What to check first                                                  |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Observational study               | No intervention, only observation                                   | Confounding and who was included                                     |
| Experiment or randomized trial    | Researchers assign groups, often with a control group               | How groups were assigned and whether anyone dropped out              |
| Qualitative study                 | Explores experiences and meanings through interviews or observation | How participants were chosen and whether themes are backed by quotes |
| Review or meta-analysis           | Combines many earlier studies                                       | How studies were chosen and whether they agree                       |
| Computer science conference paper | Short; results often in benchmark tables                            | What the method is compared against                                  |
| Humanities article                | An argument in essay form                                           | The central claim and the evidence for it                            |

### New to a field?

Start with a review article. It summarizes what is known and points you to key studies.

## 03. The three passes

Read with a pen or comment tool in hand. During Pass 1, jot down unfamiliar terms and keep going. From Pass 2 on, look them up or ask about them as soon as they block your understanding.

| Pass                   | Time                                        | What to read                                                                                                           | You're done when you can...                      |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| 1. Get the gist        | About 5–10 minutes                          | Title, abstract, introduction, section headings, figure and table captions, conclusion, and a glance at the references | Explain the paper to a friend in two sentences   |
| 2. Follow the argument | Up to an hour                               | The whole paper, section by section, looking carefully at figures and tables                                           | Explain how they reached the main finding        |
| 3. Read critically     | Several hours (about an hour with practice) | Methods, results and discussion, with the checklist below                                                              | Name the paper's main strength and main weakness |

The sections later in this guide go with the passes: use "Reading tables and figures" in Pass 2, "Reading the Methods" in Passes 2 and 3, and "Significant is not the same as important" and the critical reading checklist in Pass 3.

### Pass 1 tip

By the end of your first pass, do three things.

1. Find the hypothesis  
   and compare it to the findings.

2. Answer the five Cs.  
   Keshav suggests five questions you should be able to answer after the first pass:
    - Category: What type of paper is this? An experiment, an observational study, a review?
    - Context: Which other papers or theories does it build on?
    - Correctness: Do the assumptions seem reasonable?
    - Contributions: What does the paper add to what was already known?
    - Clarity: Is it well written?

3. Write a first summary  
   using this formula, and revise it after Pass 2:

    > In [who or what was studied], the researchers used [study design] to examine [question] and found [main result].

### Pass 2 tip

Read the Methods closely enough to understand what was done, and look carefully at the figures and tables. Then pick a key claim in the Results and find the numbers behind it in a table or figure. Do they support the claim as strongly as the sentence says? As you go, mark any references you want to read next.

### Pass 3 tip

Return to the question from the start: what would need to be true for me to believe this conclusion? Put yourself in the researchers' shoes. If you were doing this study, would you have made the same choices? Question each assumption and look for what is missing. Then make three separate judgments:

- **Validity**: Can I trust the result for the people or things studied?
- Importance: Is the effect large enough to matter, not just statistically significant? (See "Significant is not the same as important.")
- Applicability: Does it apply to my question, setting or population?

> A paper can be strong on validity and still not apply to your question.

## 05. A worked example

Here is how the three passes might go for an invented study on sleep and exam scores.

### Pass 1

The abstract reports that students who slept longer before exams scored higher. First summary: In 400 first-year students at one university, the researchers used an observational study to examine whether sleep before exams is linked to exam scores and found that each extra hour of sleep went with scores about three points higher.

### Pass 2

Table 2 shows the three-point difference, with a 95% confidence interval of 1 to 5 points. The interval does not include 0, so the result is unlikely to reflect no difference. The Discussion, however, says that sleep "improves" performance.

### Pass 3

Validity: students chose how much to sleep, so a third factor such as stress or study habits could explain both their sleep and their scores. "Improves" is a causal claim that an observational design cannot support. Importance: three points on a 100-point exam is real but modest. Applicability: one university and one year group, so the finding may not apply elsewhere.

### Revised claim

> Among first-year students at one university, those who slept more before exams tended to score slightly higher.

## 06. Reading the Methods

The Methods is where most of your confidence in a paper should come from. Use these questions to work through it. You don't need to follow every statistical detail on a first read.

In Pass 2, use the first three questions to understand what the researchers did. In Pass 3, use the rest to question it.

| Question                                              | What to look for                                                                                                    |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Who or what was studied?                              | How the sample was chosen, how large it was, and who is missing                                                     |
| What was measured, and how?                           | Whether the measures fit the question; self-report, standardized tools and records each have their place            |
| What was compared?                                    | A control group or baseline, and whether groups were similar at the start                                           |
| What could bias the result?                           | Who chose to take part, who dropped out, and whether everyone was measured the same way                             |
| What else could explain it?                           | A third factor linked to both the exposure and the outcome (a confounder), and whether the authors accounted for it |
| What was excluded or adjusted?                        | Data-cleaning choices that shape results                                                                            |
| Does each test answer the question the authors asked? | What each test compares, and whether that matches the research question                                             |

> A large sample is not the same as a good one.  
> Size reduces random error, but it does not fix a biased sample.

To see what a well-reported study of each design should include, check the EQUATOR Network reporting guidelines listed under Reading credits.

## 07. Reading tables and figures

Use these checks in Pass 2. In Pass 1, read only the captions.

You don't need to master the statistics to read results. These four checks will take you a long way when it comes to reading quantitative studies.

1. Find the sample size.

    Look for "n" or the number of participants, sites or samples. Always check the denominator: 20 people improving means something different out of 25 than out of 2,500.

2. Read the direction and size.

    Papers report results in different ways: a difference between group averages, a percentage, a correlation, a ratio. Whatever the format, first ask which way the result points. Did one group do better than the other? Does more of one thing go with more or less of the outcome? Then ask how big the difference is, in units you understand. For example, in a study of sleep and exam scores, a finding that students who slept an hour longer scored three points higher on average tells you both the direction (more sleep, higher scores) and the size (three points). For ratios, such as odds ratios, a value of 1 means no difference.

3. Check significance and uncertainty.

    Asterisks or small p-values (often below 0.05) mark results unlikely to be due to chance alone. Read the table footnote to see what each symbol means. If the paper gives a confidence interval, a narrow range means a more precise estimate. If the interval includes 0 (or 1 for ratios), the result is consistent with no difference.

4. Match text to numbers.

    Every claim in the Results should trace back to a number in a table or figure.

For figures, read the axis labels and units before the data. Ask what the figure is comparing, then what pattern it shows.

### Reading a qualitative study?

The checks above assume numbers. Instead, look at how participants were chosen, whether each theme is backed by quotes from participants, and whether the authors reflect on how their own perspective might shape the analysis.

## 08. Significant is not the same as important

A "statistically significant" result is unlikely to be due to chance. It is not necessarily large or meaningful. With a big enough sample, even a tiny difference can be significant.

Relative numbers can also make small effects sound dramatic. Suppose 2 in 100 people have a bad outcome without a treatment, and 1 in 100 with it:

- Relative reduction: 50%
- Absolute reduction: 1 percentage point (2% down to 1%)

Both are accurate descriptions of the same result. Whenever a paper reports a percentage change or "times more likely", look for the underlying counts.

## 09. The critical reading checklist

Even good, peer-reviewed papers have limits. Spotting them means you are a critical reader; you are not attacking the authors. For checklists tailored to specific study designs, see the CASP checklists listed under Reading credits.

- [ ] Does the text match the data? Claims should be no stronger than the numbers behind them.
- [ ] Does the language fit the design? Words like "causes", "leads to" or "improves" need a design that can show cause and effect, usually an experiment.
- [ ] Is it a finding or a suggestion? Authors often offer possible explanations in the Discussion that their study did not test. Treat these as ideas, not facts. If you want to state one as fact, find a study that tested it.
- [ ] Are there small errors? Typos in tables or footnotes happen. Note them and check whether they affect the conclusions.
- [ ] Could something else explain the result? Look for other factors linked to both the exposure and the outcome that could account for what was found.
- [ ] Who collected the data, and how? Ask who was included, who was left out, and whether the method could bias results.
- [ ] Is the effect large enough to be meaningful? Check the size of the result, not just its significance.
- [ ] Where do the results apply? Note the place, population and time period. Findings may not apply beyond them.
- [ ] Do other studies agree? A single study rarely settles a question. Check whether other research reaches the same conclusion.

## 10. Putting a paper in context with Consensus

One paper is one piece of evidence. To judge how much weight it deserves, you need to know where it sits in the wider research. Consensus can help before, during and after you read.

### Before you read: choose where to start

If you are new to a topic, search your question and filter the results by study type to find review articles and meta-analyses. A good review shows you what is already known, so you can tell whether a single study confirms it or challenges it.

### While you read: use the Study Snapshot as a map

Each paper's Study Snapshot lists who was studied, how many, the methods and the outcomes. Use it to find your way into the Methods, then confirm each detail in the paper itself. If the Snapshot and the paper disagree, trust the paper.

### After you read: check whether other studies agree

Turn the paper's main claim into a question and search it. Then ask:

- Do the results line up? For yes/no questions, the Consensus Meter shows how findings across studies split.
- Do stronger designs agree? Filter by study type. A finding from one observational study carries less weight if randomized trials found something different.
- Does it hold for other groups? Look at who was studied in other papers to judge whether the finding fits your question, setting or population.

### What Consensus can't tell you

Consensus shows you what studies found. It can't tell you whether a particular study was well designed. That judgment comes from your own critical reading, using the checklist above.

An AI tool can explain and prompt, but you are responsible for your interpretation. Cite the paper, not the AI summary. Finding the passage a statement came from is not the same as reading the paper, so only cite papers you have read in full and can be sure support the statement you are making. ## 11. Glossary

| Term                        | Plain meaning                                                                                                      |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Abstract                    | A one-paragraph summary of the whole paper                                                                         |
| Hypothesis                  | What the authors expected to find before the study                                                                 |
| Sample size (n)             | How many people, places or things were studied                                                                     |
| Effect size                 | How big a difference or relationship is                                                                            |
| Statistically significant   | Unlikely to be due to chance alone; not the same as important                                                      |
| p-value                     | How surprising the data would be if there were no real effect; smaller means stronger evidence against "no effect" |
| Confidence interval         | A range of plausible values for the result; narrower means more precise                                            |
| Odds ratio                  | A ratio comparing the odds of an outcome in two groups; 1 means no difference                                      |
| Absolute vs relative change | Absolute is the actual difference (2% to 1%); relative is the proportional change (a 50% drop)                     |
| Bias                        | A flaw in design or data collection that pushes results away from the truth                                        |
| Confounding                 | When a third factor is linked to both the exposure and the outcome, so you can't tell which one causes the effect  |
| Correlation vs causation    | Two things happening together does not prove one causes the other                                                  |
| Control group               | A comparison group that doesn't receive the treatment                                                              |
| Randomized controlled trial | An experiment that assigns participants to groups by chance, usually including a control group                     |
| Baseline                    | A measurement taken at the start of a study, before any treatment, used for comparison                             |
| Observational study         | A study that observes what happens without an intervention                                                         |
| Systematic review           | A review that searches for and appraises all studies on a question using a set method                              |
| Meta-analysis               | A review that statistically combines the results of several studies                                                |
| Peer review                 | Checking of a paper by other experts before publication                                                            |
